# HR & People Management — Human Capabilities

## Coaching & Individual Development

> Helping people grow — seeing their potential, naming their blind spots with care, and creating the conditions for them to level up.

- **Function**: people-management
- **Also relevant to**: education, sales
- **Why appreciating**: AI can track performance metrics, generate development plans, and give feedback on writing or presentations. The direct test now exists. De Haan, Terblanche and Nowack (Human Resource Development International, 2026) ran a randomized controlled comparison of accredited human coaches against an automated AI coaching chatbot with 114 coachees. Human coaches produced significantly higher working alliance — the trust and rapport that predicts coaching outcomes — and the trial did not replicate the more AI-favorable result from Terblanche's 2022 RCT, which had found a chatbot matching human coaches on goal attainment. The AI group's one significant edge was general self-efficacy. Read that as: AI coaching tools are useful for structure, prompts and rehearsal, and are not yet a substitute for someone who can sense that a person is stuck because they don't believe they belong in the role. As AI absorbs routine management work (status updates, scheduling, reporting), the time freed for genuine coaching remains the largest available gain in management.
- **How to develop**: Learn one coaching framework (GROW is the simplest: Goal, Reality, Options, Will) and use it in every 1:1 for a month. Practice asking questions instead of giving answers. Use an AI coaching tool to rehearse, not to deliver — the working-alliance gap shows up in the real conversation, not the practice one. After each coaching conversation, write one sentence about what the person needs most right now. Read "The Coaching Habit" by Michael Bungay Stanier — it's short and immediately applicable.

## Conflict Resolution & Difficult Conversations

> Addressing interpersonal tension, performance issues, and team dysfunction directly and constructively.

- **Function**: people-management
- **Also relevant to**: operations, customer-service
- **Why appreciating**: AI cannot fire someone with dignity. It cannot mediate a conflict between two engineers who respect each other's work but can't stand being in the same meeting. It cannot tell a high performer that their behavior is toxic in a way that they actually hear. Demand for these conversations rose in 2026 — AI anxiety and AI-attributed restructuring added a category that barely existed three years ago — while manager preparation did not keep pace. In 2026 HR survey research, 97% of HR professionals said their organization equips managers for difficult conversations about AI anxiety, layoffs and conflict; only 41% of managers said they had received that training. Spans of control widened at the same time: Gusto's payroll data shows the average small-business manager going from about three direct reports in 2019 to about six by 2024. More conversations, more people per manager, less preparation.
- **How to develop**: Practice the SBI framework for feedback: Situation, Behavior, Impact. Role-play difficult conversations with a trusted peer before having them for real. Add one scenario that did not exist a few years ago: telling someone their role is changing because of AI. Vague reassurance is the default failure mode there. After each difficult conversation, debrief: what landed, what deflected, what would you change? Read "Crucial Conversations" and apply the STATE framework. Build a reputation as someone who addresses issues early and fairly.

## Team Design & Culture Shaping

> Building teams that work well together — hiring for complement, not similarity, and creating norms that make collaboration natural.

- **Function**: people-management
- **Also relevant to**: operations, technology
- **Why appreciating**: AI screening has genuinely improved, and this record should concede that. A 2025 recruitment field experiment found 54% of candidates surfaced by an AI-assisted pipeline passed the final human interview, against 34% from the traditional process — screening is one of the places where handing work to the model measurably helps. What AI still cannot do is design a team where the cautious analyst balances the aggressive salesperson, where the new hire's energy lifts the veteran's cynicism, or where remote members feel as included as in-office ones. Trust is also a binding constraint: roughly a quarter of workers say they trust AI to evaluate them fairly, even though most employers now use it somewhere in hiring, and longitudinal evidence on whether AI-assisted hires perform better over time is still thin. Team chemistry is emergent, not engineered — but the manager who cultivates it consistently produces outsized results.
- **How to develop**: Map your current team's working styles (not personality types — working styles: how they communicate, make decisions, handle conflict). Identify the gaps: who challenges groupthink? Who keeps the team focused? Let the AI do the screening pass, then spend the time it saves on the composition question rather than on more screening. When hiring, write down what the team needs before you write the job description. Create one team ritual that builds genuine connection (not forced fun — something that surfaces real thinking).

## Change Leadership & AI Transition Management

> Guiding teams through the psychological and operational transition of integrating AI into their work — without losing trust, talent, or morale.

- **Function**: people-management
- **Also relevant to**: operations, communications
- **Why appreciating**: This is the capability of the moment, and the 2026 evidence is unusually direct. Gallup's February 2026 survey of 23,717 US employees found 27% of workers in AI-adopting organizations reported disruptive workplace change to a large or very large extent, against 17% elsewhere — and that the strongest non-technical predictor of adoption was whether their own manager actively championed it. Employees with a manager who did were 8.7x more likely to say AI had transformed their work. One correction: this record previously claimed AI's effect on boosting demand for leadership and adaptability skills is "nearly twice as large" as its substitution effect. That overstated the source. Mäkelä and Stephany's finding is that AI-intensive roles are nearly twice as likely as non-AI roles to require resilience, agility or analytical thinking, and that complementary-skill demand spills into non-AI roles as firm-level AI adoption rises. Same direction, different claim. Note also that manager engagement is falling roughly three times faster than employee engagement, so the people expected to lead this transition are the ones most depleted by it. The manager who can honestly address fears ("some tasks will change, here's how we're preparing"), create safe experimentation spaces, and help individuals find their new value proposition — this person is the difference between a successful transition and a talent exodus.
- **How to develop**: Have individual conversations with each team member about their concerns about AI — listen without reassuring. Be honest about what you know and don't know. Create a "safe experimentation" zone: one hour per week where team members can try AI tools without performance pressure. Document how roles are evolving (not just what's being automated, but what new capabilities are emerging). Study change management frameworks (Kotter's 8 steps, Bridges' transitions model) but don't follow them rigidly — adapt to your team.

## Performance Calibration & Fairness

> Evaluating people accurately and fairly — separating signal from noise, managing bias, and making consequential decisions about careers.

- **Function**: people-management
- **Also relevant to**: legal, finance
- **Why appreciating**: AI can track output metrics. It cannot assess whether someone is delivering less because they're coasting or because they're investing in a project that will pay off next quarter, and it cannot weigh the person who makes everyone else better against the individual contributor with higher personal metrics. New evidence complicates the optimistic version of this story. "Tough Ratings, Tougher Sell" (The Accounting Review, 2026) found that when managers could choose whether to follow an algorithmic performance recommendation, they used it asymmetrically — accepting the adjustments that were easier to deliver — and the bias the algorithm was meant to remove reappeared through managerial discretion. More data does not fix calibration; it relocates the judgment. The skill is not "interpret the dashboard." It is running a process disciplined enough that your discretion is visible and auditable.
- **How to develop**: Before every performance review, write your assessment without looking at any metrics. Then compare to the data. Where do they diverge? That's where your judgment is being tested. Record every place you overrode a model recommendation and why, and review the pattern in your overrides quarterly — asymmetric softening is the documented failure mode. Study the research on bias in performance evaluation (gender, recency, halo effect) and build specific countermeasures into your process. Seek calibration: discuss borderline cases with peer managers before finalizing ratings.
