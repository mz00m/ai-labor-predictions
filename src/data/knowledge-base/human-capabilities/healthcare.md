# Healthcare & Clinical — Human Capabilities

## Clinical Judgment Under Uncertainty

> Making treatment decisions when the textbook answer doesn't apply — the patient with three comorbidities, the atypical presentation, the family dynamics that change the calculus.

- **Function**: healthcare
- **Also relevant to**: legal, operations
- **Why appreciating**: AI diagnostic tools no longer merely approach physician-level accuracy on well-defined problems — on select bounded diagnostic and clinical-reasoning tasks they now match or exceed unassisted clinicians, and randomized trials show clinician-plus-AI workflows outperforming conventional resources. That raises rather than lowers the premium on judgment, because the failure mode has moved. In one RCT, AI assistance lifted diagnostic accuracy from 87% to 96% while roughly half of the remaining errors were attributable to automation bias — clinicians deferring to a confident wrong answer. The valuable skill is now calibrated disagreement: knowing when the patient in front of you contradicts the model, and being willing to say so. Regulation reinforces it. The AMA's 2026 Annual Meeting policy requires meaningful human oversight of clinical AI, and the FSMB convened a workgroup in May 2026 specifically on AI performing clinical functions with limited or no physician supervision.
- **How to develop**: Present complex cases at grand rounds or case conferences — the practice of articulating clinical reasoning under peer scrutiny builds the skill. Form your own differential before you read the AI's; the order matters, because anchoring on the model's output is the documented failure mode. Keep a decision journal for cases where you overrode the obvious diagnosis or the tool: what made you look deeper, and were you right? Study Bayesian reasoning applied to clinical decision-making. Read "How Doctors Think" by Jerome Groopman for the cognitive science of clinical judgment.

## Therapeutic Presence & Patient Communication

> Being fully present with a patient — listening to what they're not saying, building the trust that makes them tell you what actually hurts.

- **Function**: healthcare
- **Also relevant to**: people-management, customer-service
- **Why appreciating**: This needs a sharper claim than it used to. Text-mediated empathy is not the moat: a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 studies found AI chatbot responses rated as more empathic than those of human clinicians (SMD ~0.87), and in a 1,454-participant US study chatbot responses out-rated physician responses regardless of disclosed authorship. What holds is in-person, longitudinal, embodied presence — and the legal requirement for it. Seven states now restrict AI in behavioral health, with Illinois and Nevada barring AI from delivering therapy unless a licensed professional remains in charge, and Colorado and Utah among states creating disclosure and opt-out rights for AI-driven decisions in care. Meanwhile the time dividend is real but modest and product-dependent: a three-arm RCT of 238 outpatient physicians across 14 specialties found burnout and task load improved with either ambient scribe, but only one of the two cut documentation time significantly (9.5% versus control). The provider who converts that reclaimed time into genuine listening — and who patients trust enough to disclose the real symptoms, fears, and non-adherence — is doing what the transcript-level benchmarks do not capture.
- **How to develop**: Practice motivational interviewing techniques. In your next 10 patient encounters, consciously sit down (eye level), maintain eye contact, and listen for 60 seconds without interrupting. Ask one open-ended question per visit that isn't about symptoms: "What are you most worried about?" If you use an ambient scribe, protect the recovered minutes explicitly — they default back into throughput unless you claim them. Track patient satisfaction scores on your hardest cases. Take a course in narrative medicine.

- **Task categories**: interpersonal, communication

- **Confidence**: high

## Ethical Decision-Making in Care

> Navigating the moral complexities of patient care — end-of-life decisions, resource allocation, informed consent, and the tension between what's medically optimal and what the patient wants.

- **Function**: healthcare
- **Also relevant to**: legal, people-management
- **Why appreciating**: AI can present treatment options ranked by efficacy. It cannot weigh a patient's quality-of-life preferences against family wishes against resource constraints against cultural beliefs. This is no longer only a moral argument — it is increasingly the legal position. The AMA's 2026 Annual Meeting adopted its most operationally specific AI policy to date, requiring clinical AI to operate inside physician-led teams under meaningful human oversight with auditable data, and states passed roughly 14 to 15 new health-AI laws in 2026 limiting autonomous clinical decision-making and mandating patient disclosure, informed consent, and in some states opt-out rights. The clinician who can guide patients through these decisions with wisdom and compassion is also now the accountable party of record.
- **How to develop**: Participate in your institution's ethics committee or case review process. Study bioethics frameworks (principalism: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice). Learn your state's current disclosure and consent requirements for AI use in care — the patchwork moved substantially in 2026 and is still moving. For every difficult decision, practice articulating the competing values at stake, not just the medical tradeoffs. Build relationships with chaplains, social workers, and palliative care teams — ethical decision-making is interdisciplinary.

- **Task categories**: interpersonal, analysis-decision

## Interdisciplinary Care Coordination

> Orchestrating care across specialists, nurses, social workers, and family members — ensuring everyone is working toward the same goals for the same patient.

- **Function**: healthcare
- **Also relevant to**: operations, people-management
- **Why appreciating**: AI can track orders, flag interactions, generate handoff summaries, and surface relevant information. It cannot negotiate between a surgeon who wants to operate and an internist who thinks the patient can't tolerate surgery, while keeping the family informed and the patient's wishes central. The 2026 evidence base for AI in transitional care is still thin and mostly observational — a scoping review found promise alongside unresolved integration and safety questions — and adoption is the binding constraint rather than capability: in a 2026 evaluation of an AI-generated nursing handoff template, most nurses reported only slight comfort with the tool and expected moderate utility at best. Coordination remains organizational leadership applied to the most consequential context imaginable, and the tools currently assist the paperwork rather than the negotiation.
- **How to develop**: Lead one complex case coordination per month — take ownership of the communication loop between all providers. Practice the SBAR framework (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) for every handoff. If your unit uses AI-generated handoff summaries, read them against the chart before you pass them on; the summary is a draft, and you own what you hand off. Build personal relationships across disciplines: know the social workers, the PT team, the discharge planners. After each complex discharge, debrief: what fell through the cracks?

## Physical Assessment & Procedural Skill

> Hands-on clinical skills — palpation, auscultation, procedures, physical examination findings that machines can't replicate.

- **Function**: healthcare
- **Also relevant to**: education
- **Why appreciating**: Hands-on clinical skills remain on the slowest automation trajectory of any job category. A physician's hands detecting a subtle abdominal mass, a nurse's observation of a patient's gait change, a therapist's manual adjustment — these embodied skills have no AI substitute on any foreseeable timeline. The live threat is not substitution but atrophy, and it is now documented rather than theoretical. In a multicentre observational study of 19 experienced endoscopists published in The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology (2025), adenoma detection during unassisted colonoscopy fell from 28.4% before routine AI exposure to 22.4% after — a 20% relative decline in the operators' own unaided performance. Skills degrade when the tool carries them. The clinician who deliberately maintains unassisted proficiency is protecting both patients and their own automation-resistant zone.
- **How to develop**: Practice physical examination skills weekly, not just during training. Deliberately run some procedures and exams without AI assistance and audit your unassisted performance against your assisted performance — the deskilling evidence says you cannot assume it holds. Attend procedural skills workshops. Pair with experienced practitioners known for exceptional physical exam findings. Use simulation labs to maintain procedural currency. For every AI-generated differential, perform the physical exam that would confirm or rule out the top diagnoses.
