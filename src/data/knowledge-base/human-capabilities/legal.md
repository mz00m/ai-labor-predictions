# Legal & Compliance — Human Capabilities

## Legal Judgment in Gray Areas

> Advising clients when the law is ambiguous, evolving, or in tension with business objectives — the work that requires both legal expertise and practical wisdom.

- **Function**: legal
- **Also relevant to**: finance, operations
- **Why appreciating**: AI drafts contracts and reviews documents faster every year, but the earlier framing here was too generous on research. "Can we do this?" is not a question AI reliably answers alone: Stanford RegLab's preregistered evaluation of premium legal research tools found hallucination rates of 17-33%, and by mid-2026 Damien Charlotin's tracker had logged well over 1,000 US court decisions responding to AI-fabricated material, with sanctions escalating to $15,000 per attorney and the first bar suspensions — including a Nebraska attorney suspended in April 2026 after filing a brief in which 57 of 63 citations were defective. ABA Formal Opinion 512 makes the consequence explicit: uncritical reliance on generative AI output without independent verification can itself breach the duty of competence. So verification is now non-delegable, and "Should we do this, and what's the real risk?" — client risk tolerance, regulatory direction, the gap between technically legal and wise — is where the remaining value sits. Job-postings evidence (Mäkelä and Stephany, ~30 million postings across the US, UK, and Australia through 2024) shows AI-intensive employers raising rather than cutting demand for analytical and ethical judgment.
- **How to develop**: For every legal opinion you write, include a "practical risk assessment" section that goes beyond legal analysis to business judgment. Read every authority an AI tool cites before you rely on it, and keep a record that you did — courts are treating the absence of that step as the sanctionable act. Study how cases settle; understanding the gap between legal position and practical outcome is the skill. Develop industry expertise: the lawyer who understands their client's business as well as the law gives fundamentally better advice. Read judicial reasoning in appellate opinions for how judges weigh competing considerations.

## Client Counseling & Trust

> Building the relationship where clients tell you the truth — including the facts they're ashamed of, the risks they've taken, and the real reason they need the contract drafted this way.

- **Function**: legal
- **Also relevant to**: finance, sales
- **Why appreciating**: A lawyer with AI can review 10,000 documents in hours. But the lawyer who can sit across from a CEO, sense that they're not telling the whole story, and create the safe space for full disclosure prevents the catastrophic failures no amount of document review catches. AI has also added a new item to the counseling agenda rather than removing one: ABA Formal Opinion 512 treats client communication about generative AI as an ethics obligation, and self-learning tools that retain inputs can require informed client consent before confidential information goes in. So the conversation now includes what you are putting into which system and why. As AI absorbs more legal production work, the relationship — and the candor it produces — becomes the lawyer's core value proposition.
- **How to develop**: Practice the skill of uncomfortable silence — ask the hard question and wait. Build the habit of asking "What aren't you telling me?" in a way that invites honesty. Be able to explain, in a sentence a client understands, which AI tools touch their matter and what happens to their data. Invest in long-term client relationships: know their business, their industry, their personal concerns. After every client interaction, note what you learned that wasn't in the file.

- **Task categories**: interpersonal, communication

- **Confidence**: medium

## Regulatory Anticipation & Strategic Compliance

> Seeing regulatory changes coming before they arrive — and helping organizations prepare rather than react.

- **Function**: legal
- **Also relevant to**: finance, operations, technology
- **Why appreciating**: AI can monitor regulatory changes and flag compliance requirements. It cannot read the political tea leaves: which proposed regulation will actually pass, how an agency's enforcement priorities are shifting, or how a court's recent reasoning will affect your industry in 18 months. The value of that skill rose sharply in 2026 because regulation of AI itself fragmented by state rather than consolidating federally. States enacted roughly 14 to 15 new health-AI laws in 2026 alone; seven now restrict AI in behavioral health on divergent terms, from Illinois and Nevada's outright practice bans to Utah's disclosure-and-referral regime. In financial services the opposite pattern holds — no new AI-specific federal rules, with the SEC and FINRA instead extending existing supervision, recordkeeping, and marketing obligations to AI use. Knowing which regime your client is actually in, and where it moves next, is strategic intelligence that no rules database supplies.
- **How to develop**: Track regulatory proposals, comment periods, and enforcement actions in your practice area — build a thesis about direction, not just a database of rules. For multistate clients, maintain a live map of the patchwork rather than a single national answer; the answer genuinely differs by jurisdiction now. Attend regulatory conferences and build relationships with regulators. Write quarterly "regulatory outlook" memos for your clients or organization. Study the history of regulation in your area: patterns repeat.

## Negotiation & Deal Structuring

> Finding the creative structure that makes a deal work for all parties — the solution that wasn't obvious from either side's opening position.

- **Function**: legal
- **Also relevant to**: sales, finance
- **Why appreciating**: AI's role in transactions has moved past drafting. Per 2026 practitioner guidance from Sullivan & Cromwell and the International Bar Association, tools now run diligence, produce first-pass markups against firm precedent, and benchmark proposed terms against market-standard positions — which commoditizes the "is this term market?" argument that used to occupy much of a negotiation. What is left is the structure itself: the combination of earnouts, representations, and governance provisions that gives one party the control it needs and the other the economics it needs, which neither side's opening term sheet contemplated. Benchmarking tells you where the market sits; it does not tell you how to build the deal that isn't in the comparable set.
- **How to develop**: Study completed deals in your practice area — not just the terms, but the negotiation history: what was proposed, what was rejected, what creative solution emerged? Let the tools tell you what is market, then spend your time on the two or three issues where being market doesn't solve the problem. Practice reframing zero-sum issues as trades: "If we give you X, we need Y." Build a mental library of deal structures that solved unusual problems. Role-play negotiations with a colleague taking the other side.
