What's new this week:

Research and data
→ 9 new sources added: 3 Tier 1 (peer-reviewed / RCT / Census working paper), 4 Tier 2 (think tank / forecasting hub), 1 Tier 3 (Burning Glass), 1 Tier 4 (commentary). Registry: 527 → 536.
→ Tucker (US Census Bureau, QWI). Replicates and extends the Brynjolfsson-Chandar-Chen "Canaries" finding using near-universe administrative data: 12.4% regression-adjusted decline in early-career employment in most-AI-exposed industries, driven by a 9% immediate drop in hires at ChatGPT's release. Information sector: −30% early-career employment. A monetary-policy robustness check rules out interest rates as the driver.
→ Metaculus Labor Automation Forecasting Hub (launched April 20 with Renaissance Philanthropy and the Schultz Family Foundation). 93 forecasting questions with 2030 and 2035 horizons. Headline projections now feeding the site: 1.9% overall US displacement by 2030 / 3.4% by 2035; 17.2% white-collar by 2035 in most-vulnerable AI-exposed roles; 22.3% software developer decline by 2035; 52.5% / 70.9% GenAI work adoption by 2030 / 2035.
→ Liu, Christian et al. (CMU / Oxford / MIT / UCLA, N=1,222 RCT). First large-scale causal evidence of AI-induced performance decline and persistence loss after just 10–15 minutes of AI use, replicated across three experiments. A foundational methodological reference for any productivity-vs-deskilling debate.
→ Burning Glass / Levanon. Documents a structural productivity-hours break: aggregate productivity growth 1.3% → 2.2% while hours growth slowed 1.9% → 0.7%. Information sector hours −6% from 2022 peak while GDP +8.4%/yr. Featured on the homepage.
→ Centre for British Progress (UK). Comprehensive UK survey across 412 occupations and 24.8M workers post-ChatGPT — finds no detectable aggregate displacement; software sector H2 2025 decline is the strongest early signal. Notable counterpoint to the US administrative findings.
→ NBER Barth et al. (Norway). Workers in occupations declining 25%+ show 0.4 fewer future years of work and 4.7% cumulative earnings loss over 2007–2024 — the long-horizon administrative complement to the US studies.
→ Carlyle Compass AI-SaaS Survey. 26% of management teams offset AI spend via internal headcount reduction (the largest single offset category); 22% offset by cutting IT services or consultants.
→ Plus ESP / Becky Chao policy brief (citing Azar et al.'s 4.5% wage decline at AI-substitutable firms across 138M worker-firm matches), and a Slow Boring directional read.

The pattern this week is hard to miss: administrative payroll data from the US (Tucker / Census QWI) and Norway (Barth / NBER) is now showing the early-career and long-horizon displacement signal that surveys had been ambiguous about. The UK data (Centre for British Progress) is the live counterexample. The next question worth watching is whether the divergence is real — different labor markets responding differently to the same shock — or whether it's a measurement gap that will close as more countries publish administrative-data studies.
